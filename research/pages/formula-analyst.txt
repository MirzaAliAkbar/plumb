 Formula Analyst
 Race analysis for every F1 Grand Prix weekend since 2018: track replay, timing, telemetry, a tyre-wear model and race predictions.
 Like
 Comment
 Story 
 Updates 
 Submission history 
 Inspiration
 I'm a first-year Global IT Convergence student at SeoulTech. I wanted to learn data analysis and machine learning in Python on real data, not a tutorial dataset. Race timing is public, detailed and new every weekend, so every model I build gets checked against a real result a few days later.
 During the event, the Run Universe festival ran a prediction game for Aston Martin's demo run at Inje Speedium in Korea on 28 November. That gave me a second question to answer with the same data: how fast can a car lap a track it has never raced on?
 What it does
 Formula Analyst covers 957 sessions from 2018 to the 2026 São Paulo Grand Prix: 192 races, 193 qualifying sessions, sprints and practice.
 Race replay: the whole race plays in the browser at 0.5x, 1x, 2x, 4x or real time, with a timing tower that shows the interval or the gap to the leader.
 Race predictions: after qualifying, a model predicts the finishing order. A separate page checks every forecast against the result.
 Title chances: 10,000 simulated seasons give each driver's chance of the title. The app also says what each contender needs to still win.
 Teammate head-to-heads: qualifying and race record for every pair of teammates, with the median qualifying gap.
 Perfect lap and computer lap: in qualifying, the session's fastest three sectors joined up, and a lap simulation of each car built from its own telemetry. The app shows who beat their car's computer lap.
 Everything from before the event: positions, gaps, a timing screen, pace, tyre wear, pit stops, a strategy simulator, telemetry comparison, weather, race control and team radio.
 New sessions arrive on their own. A GitHub Actions job downloads them from OpenF1 every two hours over race weekends, updates the championship, retrains the models and commits the data.
 How I built it
 The app is Python and Streamlit. Every session is stored as Parquet files in the repo, 126 MB in all, so the app doesn't depend on a live data source.
 Data: FastF1 for the 2018 to October 2026 sessions, then OpenF1, with results and standings from the Jolpica F1 API. I checked my OpenF1 rebuild of the 2025 Italian Grand Prix against FastF1: lap times, sector times, tyre stints, pit laps and the qualifying results matched.
 Models: scikit-learn gradient-boosted trees for tyre wear and race predictions. Both are scored with five-fold cross-validation grouped by race, so no race is predicted by a model that saw it.
 Title chances: a Monte Carlo simulation. Each remaining race replays the points of one of the last ten races, picked at random.
 Computer lap: a two-pass lap simulation. It reads corner radii from the track outline and takes each car's cornering, acceleration and braking from its fastest lap. It is tuned on dry qualifying at the same circuit in other years.
 Replay: a canvas page in plain JavaScript, fed by lap times and the winner's speed profile.
 Charts: Plotly in the app and matplotlib for the Inje research.
 The repo has 204 tests that run in GitHub Actions on every push. I built it with Claude Code as an AI coding assistant. How every number is worked out is in the methodology .
 Challenges I ran into
 In October 2026, Formula 1 put its timing archive behind an F1 TV login, and FastF1 stopped working for new sessions. I rebuilt the download step on OpenF1, and the bundled files kept the same format.
 My first perfect lap split the lap into short stretches and took the fastest car through each one. It came out a median 3.6 s quicker than pole. Car data arrives about 4 times a second, and taking the minimum of 20 noisy times adds up. I switched to the official sector times, which put it a median 0.2 s under pole.
 Each car's distance is added up from its own speed, so it drifts during a lap. At Monza in 2026, two cars' distances were over 150 m apart. The simulation read top speed as corner speed and put Williams 7 s too fast. Lining up every lap at the slow corners fixed it.
 At first, the computer lap was more than 5 s off for 12% of cars. Most came from wet sessions or circuits whose past years disagreed. Now the app leaves the computer lap out in those cases and says why, and 0.3% of the cars it shows are that far off.
 What I learned
 A baseline matters more than a score. My race model calls 54% of winners. Pole position alone calls 55%.
 Taking the minimum of noisy numbers is biased. Averages hide noise; minimums pick it.
 Cross-validation has to be grouped by race. Otherwise laps from the same race leak between training and testing.
 Streamlit runs the whole script on every click, so caching and widget state decide how the app feels.
 Data from sensors needs checking. A track temperature sensor sometimes sends 0.0 instead of nothing.
 What's next for Formula Analyst
 Compare my Inje prediction with the AMR25's real lap after the demo on 28 November.
 Make the computer lap work in more sessions. It shows in 96 of 217 today.
 Live timing. OpenF1's live feed costs 9.90 euros a month, so for now sessions appear after they end.

 Limitations
 No live timing. Sessions appear about 1 to 2.5 hours after they end.
 The race model barely beats the grid: 54% of winners against 55% for pole, and 3.39 places off on average against 3.45. It helps most for drivers who qualified out of position: 4.0 places off against 5.0.
 The computer lap shows in 96 of 217 qualifying sessions. Where it shows, it is within a second of the real lap 76% of the time. Half the drivers beat it, so it is a reference, not a target.
 Title chances only know the last ten races. With six races left in 2024 they gave McLaren the teams' title at over 99%, and Ferrari finished 14 points short.
 Car data comes about 4 times a second, so short brake taps and exact apexes can be missed.
 The 2018 Italian Grand Prix race is missing, and the 2020 Sakhir and 2026 Spanish Grands Prix have no track outline.
 The Inje prediction can't be checked until 28 November, and a demo run won't be at qualifying pace.
